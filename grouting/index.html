Hamilton’s geology shifts dramatically from the mountain down to the bay. Up on the escarpment brow near Mohawk Road, you hit fractured Lockport dolostone riddled with karst features and old solution channels. Down by the industrial port lands and Barton Street, the profile changes to deep, compressible glaciolacustrine clays and silts with a water table barely a meter below grade. A grouting program that works perfectly for sealing rock fissures on the mountain will be completely wrong for stabilizing the soft, saturated clays near Pier 8. The key is matching the grout rheology and injection pressure to the target formation, not applying a generic recipe. We design programs that distinguish between these two Hamilton geologies from day one, often pairing a preliminary MASW survey to map void anomalies in the escarpment before any rig mobilizes.

Grouting design in Hamilton is a geological negotiation: you're balancing injection pressure against a formation that can shift from solid bedrock to a hidden ravine fill in less than three meters.

Methodology applied in Hamilton

The most common mistake we see on Hamilton jobsites is treating all grouting as simple cement-water mixes. A contractor will pump a thin, unstable grout into a loose silty till expecting it to fill the pores, and instead it just bleeds out into a nearby service trench or fractures a century-old brick sewer. Effective grouting design means selecting the right binder (microfine cement, ultrafine, chemical, or polyurethane), defining the water-cement ratio, and setting strict pressure limits based on the overburden. For compaction grouting in the loose fills behind aging retaining walls in the Durand neighbourhood, we specify low-slump mortars and stage the injection in ascending lifts to avoid surface heave. In karstic zones under the escarpment, we lean on balanced-stable grout formulations per CSA Z768, with sanded mixes to plug large voids without excessive travel. Every parameter—viscosity, set time, and injection rate—is calibrated in our lab before the first hole is drilled.

Typical technical challenges in Hamilton

Hamilton recorded a 5.2 magnitude earthquake in November 2023 with an epicenter near Vankleek Hill, a reminder that the region sits in the Western Quebec Seismic Zone. While moderate, these recurrent events can trigger differential settlement in untreated fills beneath older masonry structures in the North End. The bigger daily threat, though, is water. The city’s combined sewer system, some sections dating to the 1880s, leaks and creates internal erosion paths through granular fills. A poorly designed grouting job can actually accelerate this erosion by forcing fines migration. We’ve seen basements on the west side where uncontrolled grout injection into a leaking storm lateral pressurized the adjacent soil and cracked foundation walls. Our approach integrates grouting with subsurface drainage control, treating the hydraulic regime as part of the Improvement problem.

How do you determine the right grout mix for Hamilton's escarpment rock?

We start with a thorough review of existing borehole logs and any available Lugeon test data. If the karst features are poorly defined, we'll often recommend a targeted resistivity survey to map void distribution. The grout design then balances stability (minimum bleed) against penetrability (particle size vs. fracture aperture). For most Lockport dolostone fissures, a microfine cement with a water-cement ratio around 1.0:1 provides the right balance of strength and flow.

Can you design a grouting program for an existing basement that leaks after heavy rain?

Yes, but it's rarely just a grouting problem. We assess the external drainage, the condition of the weeping tile, and the backfill material. Often in older Hamilton homes near the escarpment, the backfill is loose and has developed internal erosion paths. We design a low-pressure permeation grout curtain to fill these voids, but we also specify surface water diversion measures to prevent the problem from recurring.
